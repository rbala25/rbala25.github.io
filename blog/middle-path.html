November 14, 2025

The Middle Path, I Think

In ninth grade I took a class called Ancient World, and one of the few things I remember from that year is this one unit on Buddhism, specifically the idea of the middle path, the notion that the good life sits somewhere between extremes and that deprivation and indulgence are both wrong answers. I filed it away and moved on. It took me until pretty recently to realize I’ve kind of been running that idea as a personal operating system ever since, without ever naming it. Although, the version I actually live isn’t quite the textbook one. My middle path was never about doing everything moderately. It’s closer to refusing to be extreme about the wrong things, while letting myself be fully extreme about the right ones.

The Grind Isn’t Free

I’ve been able to see the investment banking lifestyle very closely my whole life, close enough to know exactly what it costs and exactly what it pays. Ninety hours a week, for years, and yes, eventually a lot of money. I have real respect for the people who do it, and I genuinely understand why they do. I just know I don’t want it.

If I’m grinding a hundred hours a week at twenty-three, I don’t think I’m maximizing anything except a number I’ll be too burned out to enjoy, and I think I’d honestly be miserable in the meantime. Not dramatically miserable, just the quiet kind, where your whole life is deferred to some future version of you that never actually arrives. Time is the most finite thing you have, and happiness is my actual goal. I’ve believed that since high school and nothing has really come close to updating it. I think that’s probably true for most people, but who knows. The money down the road doesn’t buy back the decade you spent getting there, and nobody hands you a refund on your twenties. Maybe some people genuinely enjoy the grind itself, and if so, fair enough, that math works differently for them. I’m just not one of them.

There’s an old thought experiment I found in a pamphlet at a Phillies game of all places about a fisherman and a businessman that captures this better than I can. The businessman finds a fisherman relaxing in the afternoon and lectures him: work harder, scale up, buy more boats, build an empire, and in thirty years you can retire and relax by the water. The fisherman points out that he’s relaxing by the water right now. I think about that story a lot. The obvious reading is that the businessman is a fool, yet the honest reading is that most of us are the businessman, deferring the exact life we claim to be working toward, assuming the destination will still be there when we finally arrive.

Minimum Effort, Maximum Return

My default model, if I’m being honest about it, has always been something that sounds bad when you say it out loud: find the minimum effort that gets the maximum return. I know how that reads. Engineering culture, finance culture, startup culture, they all sort of run on the opposite religion, where the grind itself is virtuous, the hours are proof of seriousness, and anyone optimizing for efficiency instead of output must not want it badly enough.

I think that’s mostly cope for people who never asked what the hours were actually buying them. Effort isn’t valuable on its own. It’s an input, not a result, and treating it like a moral achievement is how people end up proud of exhaustion. Being efficient about the things that don’t matter isn’t laziness, at least in my mind, it’s the only way to have anything left for the things that do. Honestly, the phrase is probably wrong anyway. What I actually mean is minimum wasted effort, but that’s never how I’ve said it to myself.

Here’s the part that keeps the model from just being an excuse to coast: it’s a binary, not a dial. When something doesn’t genuinely catch me, I find the cleanest path through it and move on without much guilt. When something does catch, the calculation kind of just disappears, and I mean completely, the effort accounting I just described stops existing as a concept.

My high school physics teacher used to joke to my friends that he was hiding from me, because every answer he gave me just spawned more questions, and every question eventually boiled down to the same thing: why, why, why. I couldn’t leave anything half-understood. It wasn’t discipline and it honestly wasn’t ambition either, it was closer to a compulsion, this feeling that once I’d started pulling on a thread I physically could not stop until I’d unraveled the whole thing. That same mode has shown up in everything I’ve ever actually cared about, the late nights that didn’t feel late, the projects that swallowed entire weeks where I forgot to be tired. Nobody optimizing for minimum effort behaves like that. The point was never working less. The point is knowing which mode you’re supposed to be in, and trying not to let the wrong things trigger the expensive one.

Happiness is not Pleasure

The Greeks had a word for something adjacent to this, eudaimonia, which gets translated as happiness but means something closer to flourishing, a life well lived as a whole rather than a sequence of pleasant moments. I think that distinction matters more than it seems.

Maximizing happiness sounds like hedonism if you read it as maximizing pleasure, and that was never really the version I meant. A day of hard work on something you care about often contains very little pleasure and still counts as a good day. The obsessive stretches, the why-why-why spirals, none of that is comfortable in the moment, and all of it belongs in the flourishing column, at least for me. The middle path isn’t between work and pleasure. It’s between a life spent entirely in service of a future that may not come, and a life so comfortable that nothing in it ever mattered.

Which Wants Are Actually Yours

The same balance shows up in the career math, just quieter. When I first started getting into ECE, I loved the idea of power electronics, as in the real physical systems, the hands-on work, the things that exist in the world instead of on a screen. Most versions of that life that I found, though, pointed in the same direction: living in rural Nebraska, making eighty thousand a year, and the honest answer is that tradeoff doesn’t work for me. Obviously, that isn’t how it has to be. You can work in power electronics and make great money and not live in Nebraska, but the lifestyle just doesn’t match up with what I wanted, location wise especially. The work isn’t wrong, it’s just that the life attached to it isn’t the one I want, and pretending otherwise would just be a different kind of self-deception.

On the other end, I spent a while convincing myself that dev work at a quant firm is the end all be all of jobs, and the deeper I looked into it the more I had to admit it isn’t as soulful or purposeful as I would like, which sort of does matter in my mind. Nobody’s life really gets better because a market order fills forty nanoseconds faster, or maybe someone’s does somewhere down the chain, but either way it’s not the kind of contribution I’d want my life organized around. The interesting thing was realizing that the pull toward it wasn’t entirely mine. Some of it was, the problems genuinely fascinate me, and some of it was just the gravitational field of prestige and money that every ambitious kid gets caught in. Separating those two forces is a lot harder than it sounds, and honestly I’m not sure I’ve fully done it yet.

Schopenhauer had a line about how we can do what we will, but we cannot will what we will. I’d put it differently: half the battle is figuring out which of your wants are actually yours, and which ones were installed by the people around you.

Desire, Briefly

The career stuff is really just the big version of a smaller question, which is what you do with a want once you’ve decided it isn’t serving you. Everything in moderation is the standard advice, and it’s right about most things, but I think the edges of that logic are wrong in an interesting way. I haven’t eaten dessert since I was thirteen. That’s not really a flex, I’ve probably missed on a lot of good memories with people. It’s also not even for any dramatic reason, I just decided I wanted to be in better shape and it was an easy variable to cut. What surprised me, though, is that it felt like it stopped costing anything almost immediately.

Moderation assumes the desire stays constant and you just manage it forever, white-knuckling your way through a lifetime of small negotiations. Some desires you can just delete entirely though, or at least I could with this one, and once the want is gone, the deprivation is basically free. I don’t miss dessert. I don’t really think about it. The suffering was never in not having the thing, it was in wanting it, and I’d like to think the version of me that removed the want is better off than the version that would have managed it in moderation forever.

The Stoics and the Buddhists kind of converged on something like this from opposite ends of the world, that you can either chase satisfaction by acquiring more or manufacture it by wanting less. I’m sure I’m flattening both traditions by putting it that way, but the core of it holds up: one of those strategies at least has an end state you can reach. I learned the underlying concept in a ninth grade classroom without absorbing a single word of it at the time.

Holding the Frame Loosely

There’s a version of this whole essay that sounds like I’ve got it all figured out, and I want to be clear that I don’t. I’m eighteen. The plan could change, the industry I’m aiming at could transform underneath me, and the balance I’m describing is something I’ll probably be recalibrating for the rest of my life rather than something I’ve solved. The wants I think are mine today might turn out to be installed ones I just haven’t noticed yet. I think the frame is right even if the specific answers move, and I’d rather hold a good frame loosely than a bad answer tightly.

So the middle path, at least the version I actually live, isn’t working a medium amount for medium money at a medium job. That’s the misreading, the idea that the middle means mediocre. It’s being rigorous about what every path actually costs and what it actually pays, in hours and not just dollars. It’s refusing to treat either extreme as noble by default, because the grind isn’t virtue, and neither is refusing to ever go hard at anything. It’s auditing your desires and deleting the ones that only exist because everyone around you has them, then saving your full, obsessive, why-why-why intensity for the things that are actually yours. Selective intensity, basically, which I guess is a weird thing to call a middle path, but it’s the only version of it I’ve ever actually lived.

Not sprinting until you break. Not coasting until you rot. Just finding a rhythm you can hold for an entire life, and knowing exactly when to hit harder.
